And now I give the floor to His Excellency Jeffrey Hunley, Deputy Prime Minister of St. Kitts and Navies. Thank you. Mr. President, Secretary General, excellences, distinguished delegates, friends, and my fellow kitticians and divisions at home and throughout the diaspora, I bring greetings from the proud Twin Federation Island of St. Kitts and Nevis, and our Prime Minister, the Honorable Dr. Terence Drew. Mr. President, we extend congratulations on your elections as President of the 84th session of this Assembly. We also express sincere appreciation to your predecessor for her stewardship over the past year. I rise before this Assembly at a time when the world is not short of promises, declarations, or commitments. What we need is proof that our institutions can respond to the realities people face. For small island-developing states like ours, the question is immediate. St. Kitts and Nevis therefore comes with a clear proposition, trust will be restored not by what we promise, but by what we deliver. The time has come to move from promise to proof. I come not only to speak about what the world must do, but to account for what we are doing. Mr. President, last year our Prime Minister, the Honourable Dr. Terence Drew, spoke of our Sustainable Island State Agenda, CISA, our national blueprint for transforming St. Kitsa Nevis into a more resilient, sustainable, inclusive and prosperous society by 2040. Today, we return to demonstrate that it is becoming a record of delivery. Earlier this year, our second voluntary national review in the United Nations High-Level Political Forum showed stronger government coordination, closer alignment with sustainable development goals, and tangible progress for our people. Water provides one of the clearest measures of that progress. One year ago, our 2 million gallon per day solar power desalination plant was nearing completion. In February, we commissioned it. Through semi-earborne drone surveys, we also identified 17 new potential groundwater sources and in August, completed drilling at the first rural site. Together with distribution upgrades, these investments are strengthening water security. In 2022, only 25% of our households had 24-7 water. Today, approximately 75% do. This is transformation. On the island of Nevis, we are advancing a 30-megawatt geothermal power development, with drilling expected by early 2027, and Icelandic drillers contracted to begin the process. Ensign kits, plans for a 50-megawatt solar PV and battery storage project are well advanced. These investments will reduce dependence on imported fossil fuels and strengthen economic resilience. We are also strengthening food security through climate smart support for farmers and health care through our locally developed national digital health information system at the Joseph and France General Hospital. Sustainability must be practical, people-centered and built to last. Mr. President, there is more to do. But our experience proves that small states are not powerless. With investment, partnership, and political will, small nations can deliver big solutions. St. Kitts and Nevis is not seeking pity. We are advancing partnership. A sustainable island state is not a distant ambition. We are building it today. Yet, even as we advance, the climate crisis is moving the goalposts. This year, our federation and the wider Caribbean face severe drought. By the end of May, rainfall was 44% below the long-term average. We are investing in resilient infrastructure. But no small island-developing state can adapt indefinitely to a crisis the world continues to intensify. Adaptation cannot excuse inaction elsewhere. Distinguished colleagues, the science is unequivocal. The years 2015 to 2025 were the warmest on record. And in 2025, global average temperature remained about 1.43 degrees Celsius above the pre-industrial baseline, while global mean sea level stood approximately 11 centimeters higher than in 1993. For island nations, this is a question of sovereignty and statehood. If our coastlines retreat, our sovereignty must not. Mr. President, climate-induced sea level rise must never erase a state's international legal personality, extinguish its UN membership, deprive its people of nationality, or diminish lawfully established maritime zones of sovereign rights. The International Law Commission has laid foundations for continuity of statehood, sovereignty, maritime entitlements. This assembly must turn them into firm guarantees. International law has spoken with increasing clarity. St. Kitsa Nevis welcomed the International Court of Justice advisory opinion on states' climate obligations. Clarification must now become compliance. Compliments must become conduct. And promises must become performance. States must reduce emissions. Strengthen adaptation. honor climate finance responsibilities and cooperate with countries bearing consequences vastly disproportionate to their contribution to this crisis. Mr. President, those contributions least must not pay the greatest price. Our oceans must also move from promises to protection. St. Kitts and Nevis ratified the BBNJ agreement in 2025. Implementation now matters. Large ocean states need access to capacity building, marine technology, scientific knowledge and financing, while benefits from marine genetics resources must be shared freely. For us island nations, the ocean is not the space between us and the world. It is our highway, heritage, livelihood, and lifeline. Across climate action, sea level rise and ocean governance, one reality remains. Resilience has a cost. For decades, small island developing states have argued that income is not the same as resilience. Mr. President, per capita income cannot show exposure to hurricanes, droughts, pandemic, shipping disruptions, energy shocks, conflict or tourism collapse. Nor how quickly one shock can erase years of investment. This year, St. Kate St. Nevis stood before the United Nations and completed the pilot vulnerability and resilient country profile under the multidimensional vulnerability index framework. And what did we learn? We learned what our grandparents always knew. A house can look strong from the road and still have rot in the rafters. Vulnerability is not a statistic. It is a roof that was destroyed in hurricane burial. It is a school that became a shelter. It is a family rebuilding for the third time in a decade because storms have no patience for GNI per capita. Mr. President, we are not asking that gross national income be discarded. We are asking that it stop being expected to tell a story it was never designed to tell. GNI can count our coconuts and our cruise ships. It cannot count our courage and it cannot count the price we pay to simply remain. It cannot capture the cost of rebuilding the same bridge, clinic, or seawall again and again, while nations twice our size and half of our vulnerability borrowed cheaper than we do. We therefore call on the World Bank, the IMF, Development Banks, Climate Funds and every partner here to incorporate NVI into the architecture of financing, not as charity, but as arithmetic and justice made real. If vulnerability can be measured, it must be considered. Mr. President, the next great divide between nations must not be digital. Artificial intelligence offers extraordinary possibilities. St. Kitsa Nevis intends to participate, but we must ask who will shape this new age and who will live with decisions made elsewhere. At the end of 2025, some 2.2 billion people remain offline, but the AI divide also concerns computing power, data research, skills, energy, cybersecurity, and capital. With the global AI market projected to reach U.S. $4.8 trillion by 2033, developing countries cannot simply consume technologies designed and governed elsewhere. We must have a place where AI's future is decided. St. Kitsa Nevis welcomes the independent International Scientific Panel on Artificial Intelligence and the Global Dialogue on AI Governance. Inclusion must lead to capability. Developing countries need affordable computing power, infrastructure, skills and safeguards for privacy, data sovereignty, cybersecurity and rights. Mr. President, at home, we are advancing a whole-of-government technological strategy, securing digital identity and digital public services. Regionally, the Caribbean AI Task Force supports cooperation and governance, innovation and responsible adoption. Mr. President, artificial intelligence is already transforming the world. St. Kitts and Nevis chooses to help shape that transformation. As CIDS, we know a simple truth. Alone we are small. Together we are formidable. The Caribbean Declaration as a zone of peace must retain its force as geopolitical competition moves closer to our shores. Our response must remain anchored in sovereignty, territorial integrity, international law, peaceful settlement of disputes and diplomacy. CARICOM supports the International Court of Justice as the lawful path for resolving the Guyana-Venezuela dispute and the judicial process concerning Belize and Guatemala. We defend international law because for small states especially, the alternatives is a world where power becomes the rule. Across Gaza, Ukraine, Sudan, Yemen, Chad and the Democratic Republic of the Congo, civilians continue to be an unconscionable burden. St. Kitsa-Nevis affirms one standard, international law applied consistently, not selectively, and conscience supplied evenly, not conveniently. There can be no hierarchy of human suffering. Mr. President, the future of multilateralism also depends on its leadership. St. Kate St. Nevis proudly joins CARICOM in endorsing Her Excellency Caroline Rodriguez Burkett of Guyana for the Office of the United Nations Secretary General. Her understanding of the challenges facing developing nations reflect the leadership this moment demands. Her candidature as an indigenous woman from a small developing state Also, he affirms that global leadership must be defined not by the size of a nation, but by the vision, integrity, and capacity of those called to serve. Mr. President, few bridges carry greater historical meaning or future potential than between Africa and the Caribbean. The Atlantic, once a passage of forced displacement and separation, is becoming a pathway of return and reconnection. Africa-Caribbean cooperation must move beyond sentiment. Our shared history must help build a shared future to capital, trade, technology, knowledge, culture, and coordinated diplomacy. Mr. President, South-South cooperation must be felt in jobs, businesses, knowledge transfer, and opportunity. It complements, not replaces, not south cooperation and the obligations of developed country. Greater accuracy must also include justice. At the second African Caribbean summit, our leaders signed the Abiy Addisa Baba Declaration, and this year, the African Union's Reparation Mechanism and the Caribbean Reparations Commission held their first joint meeting, establishing permanent coordination. On March 25, 2026, this General Assembly adopted Resolution 80-250, declaring the trafficking of enslaved African and racialized chattel enslavement of Africans as the gravest crime against humanity. St. Kitsa Nevis welcomed this historic recognition, but recognition cannot be the destination. Repertory justice must move from recognition to remedy and from words to action. Distinguished colleagues, when we speak of repertory justice, sovereignty and solidarity, Haiti cannot be an afterthought. Approximately 1.47 million people are eternally displaced, while nearly half of the population faces acute food insecurity. In the very near future, the first round of presidential and legislative elections will take place. The Haitian people must choose their leaders freely, credibly, and safely. Mr. President, St. Kitsa-Nevis joins CARICOM in calling for the full deployment of the United Nations Authorized Gang Suppression Force and the timely renewal of its mandate alongside Haitian-led recovery and governance. Mr. President, we remain deeply concerned about the humanitarian situation in Cuba. Sinque Sanivis has contributed to humanitarian relief to the Cuban people and our own nationals studying there. We welcome the recent measures undertaken by the Cuban government to improve the well-being of its people and encourage further meaningful action. We urge constructive dialogue between the governments of Cuba and the United States. Engagement, mutual respect, and cooperation offers a best path, improving the lives of the Cuban people and advancing peace and stability throughout our region. Mr. President, St. Kitsa Nevis remains steadfast in its friendship with the Republic of China, Taiwan, and support for its meaningful international participation. Earlier this year, we joined our member states in requesting Taiwan's observer participation in the 79th World Health Assembly. We continue to support its meaningful participation in the World Health Organization, the International Civil Aviation Organization, and other relevant mechanisms, and reiterate that differences across the Taiwan Strait must be resolved peacefully. Mr. President, no generation has a greater stake in our decisions than young people. Half of the world's population is under 30. Yet 67 million young people were unemployed last year, and more than a quarter of a billion were neither in employment, education, nor training. AI, the green transition, and climate change are reshaping their future jobs. We must prepare them. Last year, our Prime Minister introduced this Assembly to our ASPIRE program, achieving success through personal investment, resources, and education. For citizens aged 5 to 18, built on savings, investment, and financial education, Aspire has opened more than 4,000 savings accounts, with our government committing more than U.S. $2 million to give children a financial foundation for adulthood. We are now piloting our financial education curriculum in schools and training teachers for national rollout. We are not simply giving children money. We are giving them the knowledge to make money work for them. Financial independence must not be reserved for those born into well. Finally, Mr. President, distinguished colleagues, for 81 years this institution has represented a bold conviction that despite our differences, nations can come together across common rules, responsibilities, and human dignity. That conviction is worth defending, but institutions cannot live indefinitely on those founding promises. They must be judged by what those promises produce. St. Kitsa Nevis remains ready to do its part and to work with every nation here towards a more peaceful, sustainable, equitable and humane world. Mr. President, trust is not something we can demand. It must be earned by promising less, delivering more, and letting the proof speak where our words once stood alone. Let this assembly be remembered, not for what was said here, but for what was done after. The tide is rising. Let our resolve rise faster still. Thank you. Thank you. I thank the Deputy Prime Minister of St. Kate's and Navy's. Thank you.
