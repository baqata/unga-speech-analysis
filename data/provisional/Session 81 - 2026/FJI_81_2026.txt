Mr. President, Fiji reaffirms an enduring principle. The international law is not optional, and peace cannot be secured by power alone. For small states, the principles of the Charter, sovereign equality, territorial integrity, and the peaceful settlement of disputes are the safeguards against a world in which the size or military strength decides whose rights matters. When the world asks small states to choose between competing power blocks, Fiji chooses principles, and we choose the charter, we choose the international law. We choose dialogue over coercion, human dignity over domination, and cooperation over confrontation. Fiji calls for the peaceful settlement of conflicts wherever they occur. We urge all parties to protect civilians, respect international humanitarian law, and return to the negotiating table. The Charter does not apply differently according to who is involved, or where Fiji asks the same of every party in every theatre, energy, food security and supply chains are inseparable from peace. The freedoms of navigation guaranteed under the international law, including the United Nations' Conversion on the Lord of the Sea, must be respected. Fiji will continue to serve in the United Nations peacekeeping operations. We ask only that they be properly mandated, properly financed and properly supported. And we support renewed investment in prevention, mediation and the quiet diplomacy that stops wars before they begin. We carry a very simple conviction. The life of a stranger is worth protecting. Dialogue is worth pursuing, and peace, however difficult, is always worth building. Mr. President, the Pacific knows the cost of militarization and nuclear testing. Our ocean carries the memory of decisions made elsewhere but suffered by Pacific peoples. That memory strengthens our determination that the Blue Pacific remain a region of peace. The Ocean of Peace Declaration expresses a regional vision grounded in sovereignty, international law and rejection of coercion. Our message to all partners is clear. The Pacific welcomes our friendship, investment and genuine cooperation. But our ocean must not become a theatre in which others settle their rivalries. For small island-developing states, regional cooperation is not an optional layer of diplomacy. It is a practical necessity separated by vast differences with small populations and narrow economic bases. And what is difficult alone becomes possible together. We must take it further still through connectivity, food and energy security, and Pacific-led financing, such as something the Pacific has built. The Pacific Resilience Facility is the first climate and disaster financing facility owned, governed and hosted by the States it serves. Its treaty entered into force in May, and it is seated in the Kingdom of Tonga. And its first grants are already reaching our communities. It exists because a village seawall does not need a three-year accreditation process. All it needs is money that arrives or will arrive. Its essential capitalization target is $500 million in the United States by December, of which $173 million has been pledged. I thank all those who have come forward and I ask those who are able to close the gap to do so willingly. Cooperation of this kind does not diminish our sovereignty. It strengthens our ability to exercise it. And Mr. President, the call to restore trust must also apply within our own nations. Fiji's democratic journey has not been easy. We have known political upheaval, constitutional change, and periods of deep division. We cannot change that history, but we can learn from it. This year, Fuji under took a national review of its 2013 constitution, informed by consultation across the country. On 1 September, the Commission presented its report to my office as head of state. that did not end the process. It began the next stage, and the report must now proceed through Parliament and the democratic processes transparently and in accordance with the rule of law. What Fiji seeks is not a constitution for a government or a party or one generation, but a settlement that every Fijian recognizes as their own. Alongside it, our truth and reconciliation process continues. Renewal strengthens institutions and reconciliation heals divisions. Together, they can carry Fiji from memory to understanding and from division to trust. Mr. President, if the United Nations is to restore trust, it must also be willing to reform itself. Fiji supports the UNAT reform, a agenda to make the organization more coherent, efficient, and effective. But reform must be judged by a single test, what improves for people and for member states. For Pacific small island developing states, efficiency must not mean distance. The multi-country officers serving our region are essential bridges between global commitments and island realities. is reform must strengthen and not thin our capacity to deliver to deliver where need is the greatest this assembly will also select the next secretary general Fiji asks for a process that is transparent and merit-based and for a secretary general who understands realities of small island states and more importantly will act on them. For too long, our circumstances have been well diagnosed and poorly addressed. The promise to deliver for all must also be measured in development. With 2030 approaching, too many sustainable development goals remain off track. National income alone cannot capture remoteness. Disaster exposure and the repeated burden of rebuilding. The multidimensional vulnerability index must move from adoption to application. There is one measure of delivery that reaches Fijian households directly. Remittances are now Fiji's third largest source of foreign exchange. They pay for school fees, rebuild homes after lots of cyclones, and sustain families between harvards. Your Excellencies, they are not aid. They are the earnings of Fijians abroad and sent home. Yet Pacific corridors remain among the most expensive in the world through which to send money. In May, in this August hall, we recommitted ourselves to bring transfer costs below 3%. and to eliminate corridors costing more than five. Pacific corridors among those Fiji us, that is to be treated as an obligation and not an aspiration. Labor mobility must also not become a one-way transfer of the people that we have trained. Fiji does not regard any Fijian the chance to build a life abroad. We ask only that those who recruit our nurses and teachers invest in the systems that produce them. And this belongs beside what I have said of reconciliation. Not every Fijian abroad left in search of work. Some left in season of upheaval when they did not feel that the country was theirs. They are Fijian still, and so are their children. A supreme law that belongs to every citizen must belong to them also. Fiji's door is open. This month, Fiji declared a national HIV emergency. Your Excellencies, one in sixty of adults is now estimated to be lived with the virus. Fewer than four in ten know that. I say this plainly because stigma and silence are now how this epidemic grew. And because of the late Rathuopilin Lailitokou, our former head of state and UNA's goodwill ambassador for the Pacific spent his last years telling us so. There is also a security question. Methametefin, trafficked into our region by transnational criminal networks, is now driving transmission through injecting drug use. Our borders are ocean. Our populations are small. And the profits at stake exceed our national budgets. A country such as Fiji of under a million people cannot interject a global supply chain alone. So Fiji asks three things. That treatment and diagnostics reach small states at prices we can pay. That the global fund and UN aid be resourced at a level that does not force trade between regions. and that our partners treat Pacific Narcotics interdiction as the shared security interest it is. These are the same laws. We are losing the capacity to staff our own future. Mr. President, nowhere is the deep gap between the promise and the delivery more consequential than in the climate crisis. For the Pacific, climate change is not a future scenario. It is saltwater entering farmland, coastal erosion moving closer to homes, and stronger storms damaging schools and roads and the pressure on fisheries and food security and communities facing relocation. This is a question of science and it is also a question of justice. This Assembly has this week addressed the threats posed by sea level rise. For Fiji, the legal consequences are not abstract. statehood once established continues. Maritime zones, once roughly established and deposited within the United Nations, must remain, notwithstanding the physical changes brought by the sea-level rise. No state should lose its rights, its identity or its city in this hall, because others have altered its coastline. Fiji Fiji urges this assembly to carry that principle from declaration into settled international law. Fiji welcomes the advisory opinion of the International Court of Justice on the obligation of states in respect of climate change. It confirms that climate responsibility is not only political or moral, it is legal. And the task now, Your Excellency, is implementation. Obligations recognized by the court must be reflected in national commitments, in the conduct of states and in the outcomes of our negotiations. Climate finance must move from promise to excess, from announcement to disbursement. The loss and damage fund must operate at a scale that reflects actual need. And excess must be simplified.
