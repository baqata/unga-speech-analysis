The Assembly will hear an address by His Excellency Christian Miskowski, President of the Government of the Republic of North Macedonia. I request protocol to escort His Excellency and invite him to address the Assembly. Mr. President, Mr. Secretary General, Excellencies, Ladies and Gentlemen, a child who will sit at a school desk for the first time this autumn, will in 2040 be a young person just entering adulthood. We do not know exactly what the world in which that young person will live will look like, but we already know that it will be different from our own. Artificial intelligence will transform professions, industries, and the ways in which decisions are made. Energy will be governed by a new configuration and a new logic of operation. Climate change will affect economies, agriculture, cities, and the movement of people. Demographic change will reshape labor markets and social protection systems. Security will no longer depend on armies and borders, but also on power grids, data centers, cables, satellites, algorithms, and trust in the information we receive every day. And yet today, as we prepare for the future, millions of people are living through war or under the threat of war. More than 100 million people have been displaced by force. more than 270 million children and young people are not in the school. This is the paradox of our time. Humanity has never possessed such immense scientific and technological capabilities. And yet, it has never been more evident that technological capability does not automatically produce political wisdom. That is why the real question before this assembly is not simply how we will manage the crisis of 2026. The real question is this. What kind of international order will leave to the generations that is entering school today? For a small-sized country like mine, this is not an academic question. It is a question of survival. Well, 33 years ago since we've become a part of the United Nations, the world looked very different. The Cold War had ended. There was hope that a new era was beginning, an era in which cooperation would gradually replace confrontation. Our region, however, was living through a very different reality. Yugoslavia was disintegrating in bloodshed. Wars were being fought just beyond our borders. The fear that the violence might spread was real. And yet, my country chose peace. It chose stability. It was in those circumstances on our territory that the United Nations did something it had never done before. It established the first preventive peacekeeping mission in the history of the organization. That experience left a profound lesson in our national memory, a lesson to which I will return towards the end of my speech. Peace does not begin when war ends. Peace begins when politics is wise enough to prevent war from beginning. Perhaps that is why small countries sometimes see international politics differently. Great powers have room for strategic shifts that small-sized states simply do not have. For a great power, a mistaken geopolitical calculation may mean a change of a strategy. For a small-sized country, the same mistake can mean a lost decade. That is why we cannot afford to be indifferent to the rules, nor can we afford to believe that the rules will defend themselves. The world taking shape before us is a world of sharper competition. Power is being redistributed. Relations among the major power increasingly shape trade, technology, energy, critical raw minerals, transport routes and digital infrastructure. What we called economic interdependence yesterday, we increasingly see today as a matter of national security. Globalization is not disappearing. It is being reshaped. Supply chains are changing. Production is once again becoming a geopolitical question. States are asking not only where something can be produced most cheaply, but who can be trusted. Not only where energy comes from, but whether it will be available when crisis strikes. Not only who develops the next generation of technology, but who will set the rules under which it will operate. Alliances are changing under the pressure of this same world. Macedonian citizens know where they belong. We are a NATO ally. The United States are our strategical partner. Full membership in the European Union is our strategic commitment. These are not three separate policies. They are one geopolitical orientation, an orientation grounded in democracy, collective security, the rule of law, and an open economy. In a time of uncertainty, our response is not strategic ambiguity. Our response is clarity. But clarity in our alliances does not mean turning away from the world. Quite the opposite. It is a precisely state that is confident in its democratic and strategic choices that can engage and cooperate with greater confidence with countries that have different histories, different interests, and different political perspectives. The world does not need yet another division into permanent camps. It needs competition with limits and rules that all must respect, alliances that take responsibility, and international institutions that remain a space for a dialogue precisely when dialogue is most difficult. That is why the United Nations matters. Not because they can resolve every conflict. Clearly, they cannot. Not because every resolution is given effect. Clearly, that is not always the case. And that should not discourage us. It should strengthen our determination to protect these United Nations and the rules upon which they are founded, and to stand for multilateralism grounded in values, mutual respect, and rules. because a world without a place where all states, regardless of their size, can sit under the same roof and invoke the same principles would be even more dangerous than the world we see today. In 1954, Doug Hammershield reminded us of a thought. The United Nations were not created to take us to heaven, but to save us from hell. Seven decades later, it is worth understanding the essence of that statement. We should not demand perfection from the United Nations. We should demand relevance. The capacity to prevent and to mediate, the capacity to protect human dignity, and ultimately the capacity to uphold principles even when applying them is politically inconvenient. Today, trust in the international system in being jeopardized precisely because too many people have a justified sense that the rules do not apply equally, that power sometimes takes precedence over principle, that commitments are conditional, that agreements last only as long as interests remain unchanged. For small-sized states, this is an especially dangerous world. Our own history has taught us that international compromises are very difficult and that peace, stability and integration require political courage and hard sacrifices. And without wishing to sound patronizing, If anyone wants to see an example of a country that has repeatedly given up something that naturally belongs to it as a fundamental right, something that many take for granted, all they need is to look at the recent history of my nation. A history marked by challenges, by security risks, of regional proportions, by constant compromises and concessions made in the name of peace and cooperation and the higher goal of integration. Often at the expense of most basic understanding of dignity and justice. We have taken truly difficult decisions. We have continued forward, even when processes lasted longer than one generation of citizens could reasonably have expected. But precisely for that reason, with the benefit of distance from those decisions and with a clear awareness of reality, I can responsibly say today that trust cannot be a one-way obligation indefinitely. If small-sized states are expected to comply with the rules, then the rules must also provide them with predictability. If candidates for membership in an organization are required to meet certain criteria, then meeting those criteria must lead to progress. If we ask citizens to trust institutions, then institutions must demonstrate that their worth has value and that their commitments endure. The rule that applies today cannot be different tomorrow, simply because the balance of powers has changed. And a commitment undertaken cannot be a temporary political convenience. It must be a foundation on which people can plan their future. We ask our citizens to believe in cooperation, in good neighbourly relations, in compromises and in mutual respect. We tell them that lasting peace is worth more than a short-term political victory, that respecting agreements is a sign of strength and wisdom, and that it is the right thing to do, not a sign of weakness. The European and Euro-Atlantic integration means accepting rules that apply equally to all. But that argument becomes much harder to sustain when citizens see that in international relations. Principle sometimes gives way to power. that criteria can change while the process is already underway, or that some are expected to demonstrate consistency that others do not always demand of themselves. We cannot tell people, trust the rules, when they can see that the rules do not apply equally. We cannot convince them that compromise is a virtue if every compromise produces a new demand. And we cannot expect to trust international institutions if citizens come to believe that their fate is created more often by the political weight of their state than by the strength of an agreed principle. This is a question of legitimacy, because our authority as elected leaders does not derive from the office itself. It derives from the people who elect us, who hold us to account, and who every day compare what we tell them with what they see. And today, they see more than ever before. We live in an age in which transformation travels around the world in seconds. Never before has humanity had access to so much knowledge, but never before has it been exposed to so much manipulation, health truths, fabricated images, propaganda, and organized disinformation. That is precisely why politics is entering a new, more difficult, and more complex era. Our task is not longer simply to inform our citizens. We must earn their trust. It is not enough to tell them what is true. We must build institutions they can trust when the truth is uncomfortable for everyone. It is not enough to speak to them about values. We must show that those values have meaning and carry costs, even when defending them requires patience, political capital, or choosing the more difficult path. We are called upon to convince our societies that cooperation is better than isolation, that rule of law is better than unilateral behavior, that compromise is better than conflict, and that institutions are better than rule of sheer force. But for the conviction to carry weight, we ourselves must be consistent. Values are not sustained by speeches. They are sustained when they are upheld precisely at the moment when it is easiest to abandon them. And many may be greatest challenges of our time. The world is changing at extraordinary speed. The centers of power are shifting. Technology is changing. Economic relations are changing. security doctrines are changing, alliances are changing, histories accelerating once again. In such periods, there is always a temptation to declare that old principles are no longer practical, that the law must adapt to power, that values are a luxury that can wait until the crisis has passed. But that is precisely when we need them most. because if in times of change we abandon the rules that protected us in times of stability, we will not gain greater freedom. We will inherit a world in which every subsequent decision depends on who has more power at that particular moment. And such a world is especially dangerous for small-sized states. That is why our duty as leaders is not to promise our people a world without injustice, without disappointment and without conflict interests. Such a world does not exist. our duty is far more demanding to preserve faith in the rules even when it is clear that the rules are being broken, to defend cooperation even when confrontation appears more popular, to sustain trust in institutions not by asking people to look less closely and ask fewer questions, but by making institutions more worthy of their trust. and not to allow disappointment with the imperfections of the international order to lead us towards something far worse, a world in which there is no order at all. That, in my view, is one of the greatest political and moral responsibilities of our generation. It applies to the international system and it applies to Europe. We will not abandon the European idea because the European path has been difficult. The European Union remains for us our natural, political, economical and institutional home. And we have to work to do at home. We must strengthen the rule of law, build more effective institutions, build a more competitive economy and strengthen our citizens' confidence in their own state. That is our responsibility and we will not shift it to Brussels. At the same time, we expect European integration to remain a merit-based process based on measurable criteria, not a process in which bilateral issues can continually move the goalposts. We are not asking for an easier path. We are asking for a path governed by rules. We are not asking for lower standards. We are asking for standards to remain standards. This is equally important for Europe's credibility at a time when its geopolitical weight will increasingly depend on its ability to translate its commitments into the tangible political outcome, and on its resolve to uphold the values it exposes and to remain steadfast in advancing them despite the many challenges it faces. Excellencies, the future international order will not be shaped only on battlefields or in diplomatic halls. It will be also shaped in laboratories, in schools, in factories, in energy grids, in data centers, and in the ability of societies to make sense of the changes that technology is bringing. Artificial intelligence is not longer a subject of the future. It is infrastructure for today, for the present. Today the world is once again recognizing the values of resilience, of reliable energy, of infrastructure, of diversified transport routes, of nearby and trusted supply chains. For us, the intensified development of Corridor 8 and Corridor 10 is a part of broader European vision to better connect the Adriatic Union, the Black Sea, the Asian and Central Europe. to provide goods, energy and people with more roots and greater connectivity, and to ensure that the region becomes interconnected in the infrastructure rather than remaining on the geopolitical periphery. Our geography does not have to be our destiny. It can be our advantage if we build the institutions and infrastructure that transform our geographical position into connectivity. At the same time, we must also approach migration definitely. Migration begins where there is no security, no employment, no education, no access to water, no prospects or no confidence that tomorrow will be better than today. It is not simply a matter of borders. Responsibility policy must bring together security, human dignity, development and the real needs of labor markets. Climate policy, too. We will have to become more grounded in reality. The green transition is essential, but if we make it social unsustainable, we will lose the political support that is necessary to implement it. If we make it technologically dependent on a handful of sources, we will create new challenges while addressing old ones. If we expect poorer countries to move at the same pace without providing them with equal access to capital and technology, we will create injustice instead of a transition. That is why future climate policy must at the same time be environmental policy, economic policy and social policy. Mr. President, all of this brings us back to the central theme of this session, trust. Trust is not a sentiment that can be created by adopting a resolution. It is an outcome. It is built when an institution delivers on the purpose for which was established. That is why reform of the United Nations is necessary, so that the organization can become more capable, so that it can deliver greater results, and certainly not to replace the responsibility of national states, but to act where collective action is needed. Multilateralism does not begin in New York. It begins when the quality of the states that came to New York. Excellencies, we are a small-sized country, but we refuse to let the smallness of our territory define the scale of our ambition. Our ambition is to become a serious state, a state that its allies can trust. A state in which young people can plan their future. A state that respects the diversity and multicultural character of its society. A state confident enough to defend its own identity without denying the identity of the others. A state that knows its history but is not imprisoned by it. And a state that does not expect others to build it in its place. That, to me, is more than sovereignty, not isolation capability. This year, Secretary General Antonio Guterres reminded us on the Charter begins with the words, we the peoples, and he added, not we powerful, not we the victors. In those words lies the reason why small-sized European country continues to believe in this organization, even when it is disappointed by it. Because the alternative to a world governed by rules is not a world in which everyone is free. It is a world in which the strongest are the freest and everyone else depends on their goodwill. Such a world may appear effective for a moment, but it is never stable. Some decades ago, the United Nations came to our country with a new idea that the international community does not have to wait for a violence to begin before it acts. Today, we once again need to say in political wisdom, we must not wait for a technology to create new inequalities before we establish rules. We must not wait for young people to live before we build societies in which they see a future worth staying for. We must not wait for institutions to lose all public trust before we reform them. And we must not wait for the world to become fully divided into opposing blocks before we rediscover the values of a dialogue. That child with whom I began will be 14 years old when the world enters in 2014. None of us today can promise that child a peaceful world, nor a world without crisis, nor a world without competition. But we can leave that child something else. A world in which power is not the only argument. a world in which knowledge is not the privilege of you, a world in which alliances create security rather than new lines of fear, a world in which technology serves humanity, a world in which a small state has the right to the same dignity, to the same rules and the same voice as the great powers. That is the promise worth renewing. Not because we are naive about the world as it is, but because we are realistic enough to understand what happens when the world is left to power alone. Macedonians and the Macedonian citizens will share their burden of responsibility. Thank you so much for your attention. On behalf of the Assembly, I wish to thank the President of the Government of the Republic of North Macedonia. Thank you.
