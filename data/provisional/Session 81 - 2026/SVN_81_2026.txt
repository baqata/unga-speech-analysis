The Assembly will hear an address by His Excellency Janes Janča, Prime Minister of the Republic of Slovenia. I request protocol to escort His Excellency and invite him to address the Assembly. Mr. President, Excellencies, distinguished delegates, Allow me to begin my speech by quoting the sentences that I delivered 14 years ago on this rostrum on behalf of Slovenia and also on behalf of a vast number of current and former heads of states at that time who signed our declaration and allowed me to quote. This was 14 years ago. This year's team of the United Nations Assembly is peaceful resolution of disputes. Last year we celebrated the 60th anniversary of the entry into force of the Convention on Prevention and Punishment of the Crime of Genocide. The Convention was the fruit of the tireless effort of the Polish jurist of Jewish descent, Rafael Lemkin. I would like to remind you that since the end of the Second World War alone, there have been more than 80 countries witnessing mass atrocities, at least 53 million innocent people lost their lives, and behind these numbers are concrete men and women, as well as children. They had a future, but they were not allowed to live that future. For this reason, I call on the Member States of the United Nations, regional organizations and the United Nations system, to develop a new approach and a strategy for the prevention of genocide and other mass atrocities. In addition, an intergovernmental forum of like-minded states should be established in order to propose a United Nations resolution that would give a clear mandate for preparation and adoption of an appropriate legal mechanism. legal mechanism that would give the concept of the responsibility to protect a new tool, a legal mechanism that will enforce the prevention of crime, a mechanism that will enable a faster and more effective response to acts of genocide and other mass atrocities. I would like to inform you that this initiative also comes from International Civil Society. the Institute for Cultural Diplomacy has gathered thousands of supporters and international non-governmental organizations among lawyers, lawyers, students, journalists and others. As early as 1946, Rafael Lemkin declared, Our heritage is the result of the contribution of all nations. All nations, as well as the United Nations as well, have great responsibility, as well as an enormous opportunity to prevent atrocities in the future. We cannot escape history, but we can learn something from it. I firmly believe that the mistakes we made yesterday, we do not want to repeat tomorrow. We took responsibility for this world from our fathers, and we have a great responsibility to make it better for our children. I call on you to improve it by joining all our efforts for a world free from genocide and other mass atrocities. End of quote. This was 14 years ago. After 14 years, I know that despite the great initial support, our initiative has not been realized. And we can all say that today the world is not better, it's worse. This initiative has been drowned in new crisis, in the incompetence and ignorance of the individuals and institutions responsible and in the double standards that have further marked the behavior of our United Nations. And sadly today, we are even further away from the goal we tried to achieve 14 years ago. The theme of this year season, therefore, is actually very well chosen. Restoring trust. A United Nations that is here for everyone. Of course. Trust begins with a simple question. Do we apply our principles consistently or only when it is politically convenient? The United Nations was founded on universal principles. Sovereignty, equal dignity, the rule of law, the peaceful settlement of disputes and the protection of human rights. But principles lose their meaning when they are applied selectively. So, Mr. President, distinguished delegates, let us be concrete. For decades, the United Nations has devoted significant institutional attention to the question of Palestine. The General Assembly has adopted resolution after resolution, while the Security Council and Human Rights Council have repeatedly addressed the situation. Let me be clear. The search for peace and security in the Middle East is of profound importance and the suffering of Palestinian and also Israeli civilians deserves serious and sustained consideration. Every civilian life matters. But we are applying the same. but are we applying the same standard of urgency when victims are elsewhere? Last year alone, over 4,800 Christians were killed because of their religion. Millions of Christians around the world suffer discrimination and persecution. In Iran, the criminal theocratic regime has brutally murdered tens of thousands of its own citizens this year alone. After 1948, more than one million Jews fled or were expelled from Arab countries, many amid persecution and violence. Or consider Nigeria. For more than a decade, Nigerian communities have faced mass killings, kidnappings, the destruction of villages and places of worship, and the displacement of hundreds of thousands of people. In return, this body produced a single Security Council resolution. Not 17, not 17, 1. So, we must ask, if every civilian life matters, why does our urgency seem so different from one crisis to another? This does not mean carrying less about one crisis, but carrying more consistently about all of them. We must also be willing to ask difficult questions about how Palestine is discussed and used politically. Majority position does not by itself determine what is right. The right of a democratic state and its people to defend themselves against terrorism must be recognized and upheld, not only in the words. Civilian suffering is tragic and must be addressed, but it can never justify terrorism or the pursuit of political objectives through violence. At the same time, criticism of government policy must never become a coward for antisemitism. If civilian lives are equal, our attention should be guided by the scale, duration and severity of suffering, not primarily by geography, political alliances, media visibility or political influence. These principles must also guide our approach to Belarus. We condemn the autoresystemic repression, arbitrary detention and persecution of political opponents, human rights defenders and independent voices. All political prisoners must be released immediately and unconditionally, and those responsible for human rights violations must be held accountable. We should speak about this. Slovenia stands with the Belarusian people and supports a peaceful democratic transformation founded on free and fair elections, the rule of law and full respect for human rights. Belarus belongs in a free and democratic Europe. Distinguished delegates, selectivity does more than distort our attention. Over time, it destroys institutions themselves. The United Nations cannot defend the rule of law while silently watching its own mandates. Procedures and institutions become instrument of political convenience. The rule of law must never become merely ruled by law. It requires rules to be applied impartially, consistently and transparently. When the same principles are invoked strictly in one case and conveniently overlooked in another, we do not strengthen international law. We hollow it out. And when institutions created to protect universal standards are instrumentalized for political purposes, we have weakened the very multilateral system they were created to defend. This inconsistency is particularly difficult to ignore when we consider Iran. Executions, including those involving political prisoners, young intellectuals and others who oppose the regime, demand serious attention, not awards, with the functions in the United Nations. And yet there is often no sufficiently serious condemnation of these executions or of the broader violation of human rights under a system, and seeks complete control over social, political and economic life. The abuse of religion to justify or reinforce such control is another example of how principles can be distorted when institutions and political power are placed above the rights and dignity of individuals. Human rights cannot depend on whether the victims are politically convenient to defend. The United Nations must therefore defend not only its institutions, but also the integrity of the principles those institutions exist to serve. Distinguished Delegates, Russia's war of aggression against Ukraine is a fragmented violation of the United Nations Charter and a direct assault on the sovereignty and territorial integrity of a member state. Russia's occupation and attempted illegal annexation of Crimea in 2014 challenged the fundamental rule of our international order. Territory cannot be acquired by force. As Mustafa Dzemilov, the leader of Crimean Tatars, reminded us at yesterday's Crimea platform summit, the international response to Russia's aggression in 2014 was too little, too late. Russia Russia was never effectively stopped. Its impunity began in Crimea. The full-scale invasion of 2022 was the consequence. The Russian leadership chose this war. Russia is the aggressor. Ukraine is defending its people and its independence. For my country, for Slovenia, sovereignty and territorial integrity are fundamental to our own security. If a powerful state can seize its neighbor's territory by force, every smaller state becomes less secure. The United Nations has documented torture and executions of Ukrainian prisoners of war by Russian forces, as well as grave abuses in occupied territory. responsible must face justice. Prisoners of war and civilian detainees must return home. Ukrainian children, forcibly transferred or deported, must return to their homes. Our goal must remain a just and lasting peace based on the United Nations Charter and Ukraine's sovereignty and territorial integrity. Ukraine must participate fully in decisions concerning its future. Any settlement must provide credible security guarantees against renewed aggression. And Slovenia will continue to support Ukraine on its European path. Ukraine's future belongs in the European Union. A permanent member of the Security Council of this organization, and trusted with a special responsibility for international peace, is waging war of aggression against another member state. This is waging war against another member state. And Russia's permanent seat cannot place it above the Charter. Its veto cannot erase aggression or responsibility for international crimes. It is ruined the world order. If this organization wants to restore trust, it should begin by applying its own principle consistently. I therefore propose two things. First, this organization should strengthen transparency around its responses to the crisis. We should establish an independent mechanism, answerable to this Assembly as a whole, that annually compares the scale, duration and severity of major crises with the scale and nature of the international response. Not to dictate political verdicts, but to make it impossible for us to remain unaware of our patterns of selectivity. And second, it should strengthen safeguards against the political instrumentalization of United Nations, of United Nations mandates and institutions. Their credibility depends on transparent procedures, consistent criteria of scrutiny for all member states. distinguished delegates the multilateral system was created to prevent power from replacing principle we must not allow political convenience to reverse that relationship so that principle becomes merely a language used by institutions when it serves political interests the answer is not to weaken multilateralism It is to protect it from instrumentalization. The United Nations that delivers for all cannot mean an organization that responds loudly to some crisis and quietly to others. We want to speak readily of the United Nations that delivers for all, and we must be prepared to ask a difficult question. Does it really deliver for all? for those living through war, for those displaced by violence, for states whose concerns receive little international attention, for families struggling to build a future. So, dear friends, we'll restore trust when a crisis matters because human beings are suffering not because the politics surrounding it are convenient. And we will restore trust when the institutions of multilateralism are strong enough to serve universal principles rather than political convenience. But restoring trust is not only about correcting what is wrong today. It is about deciding what kind of international system we are willing to pass on those who come after us. United Nations that cannot uphold its own principles has no standing to ask the world for its trust. What we owe the people of the world is not sentiment. It is an institution consistent enough and far-sighted enough to be worthy of their trust. Thank you very much. On behalf of the Assembly, I wish to thank the Prime Minister of the Republic of Slovenia.
